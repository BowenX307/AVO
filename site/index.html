Who it’s for

Every click costs something. AVO makes you pay less of it.

AVO is for people who can speak clearly, but for whom every precise hand movement is impossible, inaccurate, painful, tiring, or slow.

01 · Hardly able to use the hands

For example, after a high spinal cord injury.

One sentence replaces dozens of step-by-step commands.

02 · Able to, but not precisely

For example, with a tremor or muscle spasms.

A rough box around the thing is enough. SAM 3.1 finds the exact button inside it.

03 · It hurts, or tires

For example, wrist strain, arthritis, or a broken arm while it heals.

One sentence does several steps, with far fewer clicks.

This first version is built mainly for the first two groups.

Not the right tool if…
  • Speaking is hard for you. AVO’s main input is your voice.
  • You rely on a screen reader. AVO asks by drawing rings on the screen, and screen-reader users need a different kind of interaction.
Two moments

You say it. It points back.

01You say it.

Tap right ⌥ — a tap, not a hold — and say the task. The words appear in large type as you speak, and names on your screen help AVO hear “Smith” as Smith. Typing works too.

02It points back.

When more than one thing fits, AVO rings each on screen with a number and asks. Answer with a word — “two”, “the physics one”, “none of these” — or a number key.

You say what you want. AVO does the clicking, and checks with you only when it matters.

Why not Voice Control

Voice Control takes commands. AVO takes what you mean.

macOS Voice Control is mechanical: you think through every step and say it. AVO works from the outcome: you say what you want, and it plans the steps.

Voice Control · you drive each step
  1. “Show numbers”
  2. “Click 7” the email
  3. “Click Reply”
  4. Dictate the reply, then fix it by voice
  5. “Click Send”
AVO · you say the outcome
  1. “Reply to Professor Smith, say I’ll be ten minutes late.”
  2. AVO: Which Smith? — “Two.”
  3. AVO shows the reply: Send — OK? — “Yes.”

The Voice Control steps are an illustration. In our first scripted run of this task, AVO needed three things said: the task, “two” and “yes”. The measured comparison — what it takes to finish 12 everyday tasks with Voice Control (the fewest commands a practised user needs, done by hand) and with AVO — is in progress.

Agents ask for permission. AVO asks for understanding.

A reply to the wrong professor sets off no permission prompt — it isn’t “sensitive”, it’s just wrong. And fixing it by hand is exactly what costs the most. So AVO asks a one-word question first: “Which Smith?” — “Two.”

How it works

One sentence in. Checked before every step.

Computer-use models don’t say how sure they are. AVO checks each step before it happens, and asks you only when it should.

Several things fit

Two professors named Smith, two handouts with the same name: AVO rings each one and asks which.

An ordinary step

AVO rings what it’s about to click and counts down one second. Say “no” to stop it.

Can’t be undone

Send, submit, delete, pay, book: AVO shows what will happen — the reply it wrote, say — and waits for your “yes”.

Known limits

What AVO can’t do yet.

  • Not yet tried by the people it’s for. When it has been, this page will say what they said.
  • Relies on your voice; typing works as a fallback.
  • Needs a network connection and the Meta Model API.
  • macOS only, on the main display, and best in the browser: desktop apps are harder for it.
Privacy & safety

Your screen, your call.

01 · Data

Screenshots, and your voice while AVO shows it’s listening, go to the Meta Model API. AVO calls Muse in Standard mode, where your content isn’t used to train Meta’s models.

02 · Safety checks

Before each step AVO rings what it’s about to act on and counts down a second — say “no” to call it off. Anything that can’t be undone (send, submit, delete, pay), and any other warning from Meta, waits for your “yes”.

03 · Control

Say “stop” or tap right ⌥ any time. A spoken task ignores a stray nudge of the mouse and pauses only on a click; a typed one pauses when you touch the mouse or keyboard. Fling the pointer into a screen corner to stop at once.
