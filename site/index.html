AVO is a Mac app that works your computer for you. Say what you want done, and it plans the steps, then clicks and types on your screen. Before every step it checks that it has the right target. When two things could fit, it circles both and asks. You answer with one word.

Why I built AVO

Last summer, during my software engineering internship, I broke my hand climbing.

Writing software with one hand was frightening. Two things stayed with me. For many people, this is every day. And there has to be a better way for people and AI to work a computer together.

— Bowen

Known limits

What AVO can’t do yet.

  • Speaking is hard for you: voice is the main input (typing works too).
  • You use a screen reader: AVO asks with circles on the screen.
  • Not yet tried by the people it’s for.
  • Needs a network and the Meta Model API.
  • macOS only, main display, best in the browser.
Privacy & safety

Your screen, your call.

  • Screenshots go to the Meta Model API, in Standard mode: not used for training.
  • Anything irreversible needs you to say “yes”.
  • Say “stop” any time; if Remote’s phone disconnects, AVO pauses.

How we measured

Every number, with its limits.

Small samples, run by the author. Each says what it can and can’t show. Full tables in docs/eval.md.

E1 · Voice Control vs AVO, eight everyday tasks

1 One tester, the author, new to Voice Control; AVO’s side run by script. Read the numbers with that in mind.

Each task done once with macOS Voice Control and once with AVO, by voice: five on the demo pages AVO was built on (reply to an email, book a room, download a handout, fill an expense form, assign a card) and three new to it (a fact on Wikipedia, a search on Meta Careers, moving a file in Finder). Success was checked by the page itself, the disk, or the answer, not by eye.

On the 5 demo pages Voice Control got 2 right and AVO 5; on the 3 new tasks, 0 and 3. “Said” is the median number of things said per task, counted from the audio with Meta’s speech recognition.

Limits: one tester, the author, who has no motor impairment and was new to Voice Control. AVO’s side was run by script, with the task text standing in for speech and a script answering its questions. One run per task. A first look at the effort, not a user study.

Voice Control’s misses: dictated text that came out wrong (the reply to Professor Smith, the expense form), a wrong click nobody noticed (an extra, older handout downloaded), and steps it couldn’t do by voice at all (finding a fact on a page, a search box, a drag). On the reply to Professor Smith it took 13 things said and the reply still came out wrong; AVO took 3.

The Voice Control column on this page lists everything said in that task, transcribed from the recording by Meta’s speech recognition; the Chinese commands are shown with an English gloss. AVO’s three lines are from the recording at the top of the page.

Median things said per task: AVO 2, Voice Control 4.5.

E2 · Simulated tremor on small buttons

Rows of 8 buttons at 12, 18, 24, 32 and 44 px, with tremor of 0, 4, 8 and 12 px; 30 tries per combination, 600 SAM 3.1 calls in all, on screenshots of demo/targets.html. Clicking alone is the button’s centre plus random shake. AVO gets a shaky rough box: with SAM alone it clicks the centre of what SAM finds; with the button’s name it looks for that name near the box. The settings were fixed before the one run.

Limits: the rows on the test page sit further apart than in most apps, which makes finding a button by name easy. SAM alone sometimes does worse than clicking (a 32 px button at 8 px of tremor: 93% alone, 80% with SAM). Clicking alone is computed, so it doesn’t count the time a click takes. Data and scripts.

33% vs 100%: a 12 px button with 8 px of simulated tremor, hit when clicking alone vs with AVO, which finds the button by its name. When the app names its buttons (most do), AVO doesn’t need to aim at all: you box roughly and say the name. 33% vs 47%: the same with AVO aiming with SAM 3.1 only, for buttons without a name.

E3 · Finished wrong without asking

Three demo tasks, each with a real ambiguity: two Professor Smiths, “next Friday” said on a Saturday, two near-identical handouts. Three setups, 3 runs each per task: AVO as built; an agent that asks only before sending or booking; the agent alone. A script answered for the user, the way the task meant it. A run counts when it ends with the wrong result and reports it as done, without having asked anything.

Limits: 9 runs per setup, so a direction, not a rate. All three setups plan first, and the plan sometimes asks a question, so “the agent alone” isn’t completely silent. The tasks were chosen to contain an ambiguity.

Why it matters: a reply to the wrong professor sets off no permission prompt, and fixing it by hand is exactly what costs the most. So AVO asks a one-word question first (“Which Smith?” “Two.”). Across these runs AVO asked about one more thing per task.

Known limits, in full

Speaking is hard for you: AVO’s main input is your voice. You rely on a screen reader: AVO asks by drawing rings on the screen, and screen-reader users need a different kind of interaction. Not yet tried by the people it’s for; when it has been, this page will say what they said. Relies on your voice; typing works as a fallback (click the island). Needs a network connection and the Meta Model API. macOS only, on the main display, and best in the browser: desktop apps such as Finder are harder for it. Each task stops at 20 steps or $0.50 of API calls.
